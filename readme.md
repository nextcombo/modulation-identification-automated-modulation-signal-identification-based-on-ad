# [Modulation Identification] Automated Modulation Signal Identification Based on Adaptive Wavelet Networks [with code attached]

Introduction With the rapid development of modern communication technologies, modulation methods in wireless communications are continuously increasing in diversity and complexity. Additionally, the growing demand for users and explosive data volumes have led to increasingly complex communication environments. Therefore, it is crucial to rapidly and automatically identify and classify communication signals. Automated Modulation Classification (AMC) plays a vital role in key processes in wireless communications, serving a wide range of civilian and military applications such as cognitive radio, electronic warfare, spectrum monitoring, surveillance analysis, and radio fault detection. It serves as a bridge between signal detection and demodulation, effectively classifying the types of signal modulations received. Thus, having a trustworthy and effective AMC plan is essential.
In addition to using I/Q metadata or spectrum as inputs, many works have also incorporated some manual features along with I/Q metadata to enhance performance. For example, Qi et al. [34] implemented multimodal inputs (amplitude, phase, Fourier features, cumulants, etc.) to improve performance. Huynh-The et al. [35] and Zhang et al. [36] connected I/Q data with amplitude and phase, providing an effective representation of the signal. Moreover, Kim et al. [37] proposed a two-stage method that uses one network to convert I/Q signals into image data, and another network to classify these images through these data. Although these methods are effective, they require manual introduction of handcrafted features and are not conducive to end-to-end deployment.
Due to the need for large amounts of data in training models in AMC, a number of researchers have begun addressing data security and privacy concerns. To collaborate on datasets from various devices without disclosing device information, Shi et al. [38] integrated joint learning into AMC to improve accuracy while maintaining data security. Liu et al. [39] proposed a blockchain federated learning framework and designed a method for evaluating parameter effectiveness, significantly reducing the impact of malicious nodes. Liu et al. [40] introduced a novel feature aggregation technique to enhance accuracy, similar to the model average. These works have propelled the implementation of deep learning models at an industrial level on AMC.
As a typical classification problem within deep learning, this paper also discusses the issue of data augmentation in the AMC task. Many studies [41], [42], [43] have also suggested using generative adversarial networks (GANs) for data augmentation to address the issue of insufficient labeled data. Zheng et al. [44] presented a strategy for converting I-Q signals into amplitude spectra and applying random masking to the spectra before converting them back to the time domain for data augmentation. Huang et al. [45] studied three typical augmentation methods: rotation, flipping, and Gaussian noise. Herwaman et al. [46] enhanced the training phase by adding Gaussian noise to the classifier layer to achieve implicit regularization. These data augmentation methods alleviated the difficulties in training AMC models and avoided overfitting.
From a more diverse and creative perspective, AMC is considered. Complex recursive convolution, batch normalization, weight initialization, and dense methods are described in [47]. Tu et al. [48] attempt to fuse different images and handcrafted signal features to obtain more discriminative features, indicating superior performance compared to non-fusion techniques. Rajendran et al. [49] propose the problem of modulation classification for distributed wireless spectrum sensing networks. Ramjee et al. [50] present an overly complex greedy search algorithm seeking the optimal solution. Abdelbar et al. [51] introduce cooperative AMC (CAMC), where signals from unknown transmitters with many sensors outperform AMC using a single sensor. Cai et al. [52] apply Transformer Networks to AMC and combine global information of each sample sequence. Tian et al. [53] propose a modulation constraint (MC) cluster classifier to identify signals. Chen et al. [54] design a trainable operator that converts raw signals into square matrices, then classifies them using typical CNNs. Dong et al. [55] apply semi-supervised methods to alleviate dependence on labeled training samples in AMC. Zhang et al. [56] apply parameter estimators and parameter Transformers to improve classification accuracy. Huang et al. [57] introduce a novel loss function with contrastive loss, which is introduced to train models, thereby enhancing the differences between various modulation modes. Doan et al. [58] propose a multi-task network for simultaneous modulation classification and DOA estimation. Huynh-The et al. [59] apply AMC methods to real scenarios in intelligent radar systems. Huynh-The et al. [60] study the modulation classification of damaged OFDM signals. The above technologies are pushing the expansion of AMC deep learning.

We applied an enhancement scheme to perform adaptive wavelet decomposition on the feature map to extract more information. Compared with the widely used CNN and RNN architectures, our proposed multi-level decomposition for the first time considers the inherent frequency domain characteristics of the signal.
2) To integrate cross-channel information from different decomposition levels, a simple and effective attention mechanism was applied. It enables the network to focus on different decomposition levels by applying different weights to the feature vectors of different channels. This allows our model to assign different weights to multiple frequency bands instead of focusing solely on individual frequencies as in previous works.
3) Finally, we compared our method with existing benchmark models on three datasets, and experimental results show that this approach has good performance. At the same time, we discussed the sensitivity of hyperparameters such as the number of decomposition layers and computational complexity comprehensively, and verified the effectiveness of the model through burn-in experiments. Finally, through visual analysis, we discussed in detail the results of the multi-level decomposition.
Conclusion: This paper proposes AWN, which combines multi-level decomposition modules. It can integrate semantic information in the feature map by performing adaptive wavelet decomposition. An attention mechanism is introduced to integrate cross-channel information from different decomposition levels. Compared with previous architectures, our proposed AWN leverages the first functionality of signals across different frequency bands.
Through experiments on RML2016.10a, RML2016.10b, and RML2018.01a, we found that AWN outperformed other baseline models on three datasets and had relatively low computational complexity. This indicates that the implementation of multi-level decomposition indeed benefits signal classification. The effects of the ablations process and sensitive experiments on classification performance were explored. Visual analysis visualized the decomposed feature maps to gain intuitive understanding. All these experiments and analyses demonstrate the application prospects of adaptive wavelets in AMC.
